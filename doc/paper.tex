\section*{Abstract}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

SuperMoTo is an open-source audio plugin (VST3, AU and Standalone) that puts a studio's monitoring signal chain into one plugin instance. From that instance it routes up to 32 inputs to 32 outputs through a configurable gain-and-EQ matrix, applies per-loudspeaker FIR correction filters derived from in-room acoustic measurements, time-aligns the loudspeakers of a rig and phase-aligns a subwoofer to the corrected main speakers, and generates periphonic Ambisonics decoders up to third order for immersive listening rooms. Acoustic measurement tools and a transfer-function analysis and correction design engine are built in, so the whole calibration workflow happens inside the plugin, one loudspeaker at a time or for a whole rig in a single pass. It is implemented in C++ using the JUCE framework and is licensed under the AGPL~3.0, or under commercial terms for holders of a commercial JUCE licence.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Decisions about balance, timbre and spatial imagery are all made at the listening position, so a systematic error in what the engineer hears accumulates through the whole mix. In a real room, what the engineer hears is never the loudspeaker alone but the combined response of the loudspeaker, the room, and any bass-management or routing in the signal path. Three independent classes of error contribute to this. Room modes below the Schroeder frequency produce large, position-dependent peaks and dips with audible ringing, speaker-boundary interference and early reflections add comb-filtering through the midrange, and the loudspeaker's own magnitude and phase response is rarely flat \cite{Toole}. Electronic correction cannot cure all of these (room cancellation notches and late reflections are not minimum-phase and cannot be inverted by any causal filter~\cite{NeelyAllen}), but the broad, reproducible, minimum-phase portion of the combined loudspeaker-room response can be usefully attenuated with a well-designed FIR filter, which reduces coloration and improves translation to other playback systems.

No single open-source tool covers this workflow end to end. The widely-used Room EQ Wizard (REW)\footnote{\url{https://www.roomeqwizard.com/}} provides acoustic measurement and filter design as a standalone desktop application but not as a DAW plugin, requiring the resulting correction impulse responses to be loaded into a separate convolution plugin and routing to be managed outside the tool. Commercial room correction products such as Sonarworks SoundID Reference\footnote{\url{https://www.sonarworks.com/soundid-reference}} and IK Multimedia ARC\footnote{\url{https://www.ikmultimedia.com/products/arcstudio/}} focus exclusively on correction and do not integrate routing, bass management, or immersive audio decoding. High-channel routing tools and Ambisonics decoders exist as separate plugins, the IEM Plug-in Suite\footnote{\url{https://plugins.iem.at/}, GPLv3, from the Institute of Electronic Music and Acoustics, Graz.} being the reference open-source example, but are not combined with a calibration workflow. SuperMoTo covers the whole workflow in one plugin instance, namely the monitoring matrix, the measurement engine, the correction design, the FIR convolution and the Ambisonics decoder generation. It can also read the decoding matrices exported by IEM's AllRADecoder, so an all-round decoder can be driven by a set of measured, individually corrected loudspeakers. The combination matters most in VR and immersive audio production, where a loudspeaker array has to be routed, time-aligned, individually corrected and driven by a decoded Ambisonics signal at the same time.

Aligning a subwoofer's phase to the mains is itself established practice rather than a new idea, and Section~\ref{sec:subalign} places the construction used here against the parametric all-pass designs it descends from. The contributions of this paper are the following.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A monitoring chain that carries routing, per-loudspeaker EQ, time alignment, FIR correction and Ambisonics decoding in one plugin instance, with a latency budget that keeps a rig aligned exactly as the user set it for the least added latency (Section~\ref{sec:latency}).
  \item A closed-form subwoofer phase alignment, read directly off the measured phase and carried by the correction filter that is already there, in place of a parametric all-pass fitted by an optimiser, as done by other solutions (Section~\ref{sec:subalign}).
  \item A power-weighted crossover-band group-delay estimator for the main-to-subwoofer offset, and the identity that makes one such offset per loudspeaker correct across a whole rig sharing a single subwoofer (Sections~\ref{sec:subalign} and~\ref{sec:group}).
  \item An all-in-one open-source implementation in which the measurement, the analysis, the filter design and the playback chain are the same instance, so a rig can be calibrated without leaving it (Section~\ref{sec:impl}).
  \item A selectable monitor target curve, either a factory curve informed by EBU Tech 3276, ITU-R BS.1116-3 and preference studies or a user-defined tilt, applied identically on every output so that it cancels out of the main-to-subwoofer phase relationship (Section~\ref{sec:chain}).
\end{itemize}

\section{The monitoring chain}\label{sec:chain}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The routing matrix connects up to 32 inputs to 32 outputs through a grid of crosspoint \emph{frames}. Six independent configurations (A--F) are stored per preset and can be engaged in exclusive mode (instant A/B switching between monitoring rigs) or summed. Everything the plugin does to the audio happens in one pass over the block, in the order the signal physically travels. With $X_i$ the inputs and $\omega = 2\pi f$, the signal reaching output $o$ is
\begin{equation}
  Y_o(\omega) \;=\; g\;
    \underbrace{e^{-j\omega k_o/f_s}}_{\text{latency comp.}}\;
    \underbrace{\tau_o\,E_o(\omega)\,T(\omega)\,e^{-j\omega \delta_o/f_s}\,C_o(\omega)}_{\text{loudspeaker }o}\;
    \sum_{c\,\in\,\mathcal{A}}\;\sum_{i}\;
    \underbrace{\gamma_{cio}\,B_{cio}(\omega)}_{\text{crosspoint}}\;X_i(\omega),
  \label{eq:chain}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of engaged configurations, $k_o$ and $\delta_o$ are delays in samples at the sampling rate $f_s$, $T(\omega)$ is an optional monitor target curve shared unmodified by every output, and $g$ is the master gain (level, mute and dim on one smoother).

A crosspoint contributes a gain and polarity $\gamma_{cio}$, ramped linearly over \SI{50}{\milli\second} so that switching a route, or a whole configuration, cannot click, followed by its own two-band EQ $B_{cio}$, which is where a configuration-specific tweak on that one route belongs. The per-output terms are the trim and polarity $\tau_o$, a second two-band EQ $E_o$, the target curve $T$, a delay of $\delta_o$ samples (up to \SI{100}{\milli\second}) for physical speaker time-alignment, and the FIR correction $C_o$, applied by a zero-latency partitioned convolution engine \cite{Gardner1995}. Each EQ band is a peaking biquad, or a lowpass, highpass or bandpass of second or fourth order, a fourth-order section being two biquads carrying the Butterworth $Q$ pair scaled by the band's own $Q$ setting.

The FIR correction $C_o$ is designed to flatten the measured response, which is a target in itself: a loudspeaker corrected flat at the listening position sounds harsh, because its directivity index rises with frequency \cite{ITU1116} and the flattened measurement has therefore lifted the direct sound in the treble by the amount the room's reverberant field would otherwise have rolled off. $T$ is where that is put right: a gentle downward tilt from \SI{0}{\dB} at \SI{20}{\hertz} to a user-chosen fall (typically \SIrange{-3}{-6}{\dB}) at \SI{20}{\kilo\hertz}, picked from factory curves informed by EBU Tech 3276's operational room response mask \cite{EBU3276}, ITU-R BS.1116-3 \cite{ITU1116} and the Harman in-room preference target \cite{Olive2013,Toole2015}, or defined by the user as a straight tilt, an EBU-like turnover, or a low-shelf bass lift. Because $T$ carries no output index $o$, it is identical on the mains and the subwoofer, so it cancels out of the phase relationship the alignment of Section~\ref{sec:subalign} depends on, where a tilt folded into each output's own correction filter $C_o$ instead would not.

The delay is rounded to a whole number of samples and read from a ring buffer at one tap, so that it is a pure sample shift and the same operation on every output. The half sample given up is \SI{11}{\micro\second} at \SI{44.1}{\kilo\hertz}, \SI{4}{\milli\metre} of path, and one sample of relative error between two outputs places its first cancellation notch at $f_s/2$, so it cannot comb inside the band. Interpolating between two taps instead, as this did until the measurements of Section~\ref{sec:validation} showed it up, colours the treble by where the delay falls between samples --- \SI{1.5}{\dB} at \SI{8}{\kilo\hertz} and \SI{7.6}{\dB} at \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz} for half a sample, and differently on each output. A windowed-sinc interpolator removes that colouration at about ten times the arithmetic and is kept in the source for a rig that needs the resolution; for a listening room, rounding removes it at none.

Everything that describes a physical loudspeaker therefore multiplies once, after the sum, and is shared by every configuration (A...F) that drives it, while the routing and its per-route tweaks belong to one configuration. When filters of different lengths are loaded on different outputs, automatic inter-output latency compensation keeps every speaker time-aligned at the listening position for the least added latency (Section~\ref{sec:latency}).

A configuration tool generates standard stereo and surround layouts (2.0 through 7.1) with bass management and writes a complete matrix configuration ready for refinement. The subwoofer is fed either from a dedicated input (an LFE channel) or from the sum of the main channels, and bass management is written as a highpass on each main output and a lowpass on the subwoofer's.

\section{Latency and time alignment}\label{sec:latency}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The partitioned convolution engine adds no block latency whatever the filter length, but a linear-phase correction is centred on its own impulse, so output $o$ carries a bulk latency $\ell_o$ equal to the position of the peak of its loaded impulse response (about half the filter length, and near zero for a minimum-phase filter). Filters of different lengths on different outputs would therefore introduce timing inconsistencies between them. Writing $d_o$ for the delay the user (or automatic analysis) set on output $o$ in samples and $(\cdot)_+$ for the positive part, the delay line actually runs only the remainder $\delta_o = (d_o-\ell_o)_+$, the uniform compensation is
\begin{equation}
  k \;=\; \max_{o\ \mathrm{fed}}\;\bigl(\ell_o - d_o\bigr)_+ ,
\end{equation}
and each fed output takes the share $k_o = d_o + k - \max(d_o,\ell_o)$ of it, so that
\begin{equation}
  \delta_o + \ell_o + k_o \;=\; d_o + k
  \qquad \text{on every fed output.}
\end{equation}
The rig is aligned exactly as wanted, offset by a single common term $k$, which is the smallest value satisfying $d_o + k \ge \ell_o$ everywhere and is zero whenever each output's own alignment delay already covers its own filter, the usual case for a rig that has been time-aligned. An output that no engaged configuration feeds is left out of the maximum, so an unused loudspeaker's long filter cannot impose latency on the rig.

\section{Ambisonics decoding}\label{sec:ambi}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The configuration tool also generates periphonic (3D) Ambisonics sampling decoders for orders 1 through 3, following the AmbiX convention (ACN channel order, SN3D normalisation) \cite{NachbarAmbiX2011}. For each loudspeaker at azimuth $\theta$, elevation $\varphi$ and radius $r$, the decode gain is
\begin{equation}
  D[s][j] \;\propto\; a_{n(j)}\,Y_j^{\mathrm{SN3D}}(\Omega_s),
\end{equation}
where $a_n$ are the max-$r_E$ per-degree weights derived from the largest root of the Legendre polynomial $P_{N+1}$ \cite{ZotterFrank2019}, which improves high-frequency localisation stability. The rig is described by a loudspeaker count and a table of azimuth, elevation, radius and output channel, starting either from a hand-tuned regular layout for the order's canonical count or from a near-uniform spherical Fibonacci lattice for any other count. Non-equidistant speaker rigs are compensated by per-speaker delay and trim derived from the inverse-square law, each of which can be left out so that an alignment or a level already obtained from measurements survives a later re-application of the decoder. Bass management low-passes the omnidirectional $W$ component to a subwoofer and high-passes the speaker feeds.

Irregular and hemispherical rigs are better served by an all-round decoder than by a plain sampling decoder, so the tool can instead import the \texttt{.json} configuration exported by the IEM AllRADecoder and use its matrix, converting it to the plugin's SN3D/ACN feed and applying the same radius compensation.

\section{Acoustic measurement}\label{sec:measurement}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The measurement view drives the plugin outputs with a band-limited stimulus (\SIrange{5}{30}{\second} duration) and records the microphone response on one of the plugin's input channels. The sweep stimulus is a \emph{synchronized} exponential sweep in the sense of \cite{Novak2015}, its sweep rate being quantised so that the stimulus phase at each harmonic arrival time is an exact multiple of $2\pi$, which is what allows the distortion orders to be separated with their true phase at analysis time (Section~\ref{sec:correction}). White noise is offered as the alternative stimulus.

Each captured stereo file stores the raw stimulus on channel 1 and the microphone on channel 2, so the transfer function estimate at analysis time is always the ratio of the captured microphone signal to the sent stimulus. Three modes are provided, "Dry" (bare loudspeaker and room, correction bypassed), "FIR" (single output with its correction in circuit, for before and after verification), and "System" (complete engine including routing and all FIR filters, for end-to-end validation).

A run measures every selected channel in turn with the microphone in one place and writes its files into a measurement folder that numbers positions per channel automatically and tags an optional subwoofer channel. The folder carries a generated XML manifest recording, for every run, its time, its stimulus parameters, a free-text comment, the microphone calibration in effect (name and a verbatim copy of the file) and the dB SPL calibration if one has been made by reading a real sound level meter. The folder is therefore self-describing, and the group-alignment mode of Section~\ref{sec:group} loads one in a single step. Microphone frequency-response calibration files in the REW/miniDSP plain-text format are supported and are applied at analysis time rather than baked into the recordings, so the calibration can be updated, or replaced by the copy embedded in a folder measured years earlier, without re-measuring.

\section{Transfer-function estimation and correction design}\label{sec:correction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For each set of measurements (one speaker, multiple microphone positions), the plugin offers two estimators for the loudspeaker-room transfer function. The first is the Welch $H_1$ estimator \cite{Welch1967}
\begin{equation}
  \hat H(f) = \frac{\hat P_{xy}(f)}{\hat P_{xx}(f)},
\end{equation}
with Hann-windowed 50\,\%-overlapped segments and a user-selectable window length of 16\,384 to 262\,144 samples, and it applies to any stimulus. The second, available and selected by default when the run's manifest identifies it as a synchronized sweep, deconvolves the recording by the analytic spectral inverse of the sweep \cite{Novak2015}, which yields the full-band response from a single sweep and requires no regularised division by a measured spectrum. Because a distortion product of order $m$ is emitted $L\ln m$ seconds earlier than the linear response at the same frequency ($L$ being the sweep rate), the deconvolved response separates the orders along the time axis. The linear impulse response is windowed out for the estimate, and orders 2 to 5 are retained as distortion curves, power-averaged across positions. Under the Welch estimator those same products are instead folded into the linear estimate. The two methods share the entire downstream chain, so a set can be re-analysed either way for comparison.

Before averaging across positions, the bulk propagation delay of each measurement is estimated from the impulse response peak and removed, so position-dependent phase ramps do not cancel in the spatial average. The resulting complex spatial average follows the practice of correcting the broad trend common to the listening area rather than a single-seat interference pattern \cite{Toole, Mourjopoulos1994}.

The correction filter is the regularised complex inverse of the frequency-dependent-smoothed average. A variable fractional-octave smoothing is applied, where the octave fraction interpolates log-linearly from an independent low-frequency setting to a high-frequency setting, allowing the correction to track room modes finely at low frequencies while responding only to broad trends above a few kilohertz \cite{Hatziantoniou2000}. Boosts are limited by a smooth $\tanh$ soft-knee ceiling to avoid ringing and headroom loss at room cancellation notches \cite{Kirkeby1999}, and the correction fades back to unity outside a user-set analysis range, where neither the loudspeaker nor the measurement carries usable information.

For subwoofer-equipped systems, a second set of measurements (the sub at the same microphone positions) is loaded, and the phase of the main's correction is steered onto the subwoofer's across the crossover region by an all-pass factor, so that the two sum coherently there. That design is developed in Section~\ref{sec:subalign}.

The final correction spectrum is exported as a mono 32-bit float impulse-response wav file in either \emph{linear-phase} (energy centred at $N/2$, enabling full phase and subwoofer alignment at the cost of $N/2$ samples of latency) or \emph{minimum-phase} (real-cepstrum method, magnitude-only correction with essentially zero bulk latency, suited to tracking sessions) form \cite{OppenheimSchafer}.

\section{Subwoofer phase alignment}\label{sec:subalign}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

A main loudspeaker and a subwoofer only sum to a flat response through the crossover when they are in phase there \cite{Linkwitz1976}. A fourth-order Linkwitz-Riley lowpass contributes a group delay of $2\sqrt{2}/\omega_x$ at DC and at its corner, \SI{5.6}{\milli\second} at $f_x=\SI{80}{\hertz}$, and the low-frequency acoustic centre of a loudspeaker moves with frequency as well \cite{Vanderkooy2006}. Several milliseconds of the offset are therefore built into the system, and no repositioning of the box removes them.

Steering a main's phase onto the subwoofer's through the crossover has been proposed by a few authors or constructors. Audyssey's phase equalisation cascades second-order all-pass sections onto the satellite channel and computes their pole angles and radii recursively, so as to minimise $\varphi_{\mathrm{sub}}-\varphi_{\mathrm{sat}}-\varphi_{\mathrm{AP}}$ over a weighted band around the crossover \cite{Bharitkar2010, BharitkarBook}, and Dirac Live Bass Control applies a pre-specified number of all-pass biquads to the high- and low-frequency branches so that the subwoofer and the channel are in phase around the crossover at a chosen position in the room \cite{DiracBass}. The crossover region remains an active subject \cite{Kim2025}. In each of these the all-pass is a parametric filter of chosen order whose coefficients are found by an optimiser against a phase-error criterion. What follows shares that objective and differs in construction. The alignment is read straight off the measured phase in closed form, on the same frequency grid the correction is designed on, so there is no filter order to choose, no objective function and no convergence to check, and it is carried by the FIR that already applies the magnitude correction, so it costs nothing at run time.

Aligning the two first requires their relative timing to survive the analysis, which by default it does not. The bulk propagation delay of each measurement is estimated from its impulse-response peak and removed before the positions are averaged, since otherwise the position-dependent phase ramps cancel in the average. Each subwoofer capture is instead delay-anchored on the main capture taken at the same position, shifted by the main's delay rather than by its own, so the averaged subwoofer response $\bar S$ keeps its true phase relative to the main's average.

The alignment itself is a single factor applied to the correction spectrum. From the subwoofer's unit-magnitude phasor $\hat s = \hat S/|\hat S|$, where $\hat S(f) = \pm\,\bar S(f)\,e^{\,j2\pi f T}$ carries the subwoofer polarity and an assumed bulk delay $T$ discussed below, the correction $C$ designed for the main is multiplied by
\begin{equation}
  A(f) \;=\; \frac{\bigl(1-w_x(f)\bigr) + w_x(f)\,\hat s(f)}
                  {\bigl|\bigl(1-w_x(f)\bigr) + w_x(f)\,\hat s(f)\bigr|},
  \label{eq:allpass}
\end{equation}
where the alignment weight is a raised cosine in log frequency,
\begin{equation}
  w_x(f) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cos\bigl(\pi\, x(f)\bigr),
  \qquad
  x(f) \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\ \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{oct}}}\log_2\frac{f}{f_x}\right)\right),
  \label{eq:alignweight}
\end{equation}
so $w_x=1$ at and below the crossover $f_x$ and $w_x=0$ at and above $2^{N_{\mathrm{oct}}} f_x$, with a release width $N_{\mathrm{oct}}$ of one octave by default. Above the release the main dominates and keeps the phase its own correction gives it.

Three properties of \eqref{eq:allpass} are what make it usable. Taking the phasor discards the subwoofer's magnitude, so none of the subwoofer's own response leaks into the main's correction. The renormalisation makes $|A|=1$ at every bin, so the alignment is strictly a phase operation and leaves the designed magnitude correction untouched, which is what allows the same design to serve with and without a subwoofer. And the blend is taken on the phasors rather than on an unwrapped phase angle, so the interpolation travels the short arc between $1$ and $\hat s$ and the rotation $A$ applies is bounded by $\pi$ at every frequency, by construction and with no phase-unwrapping step, which would need an anchor in a band where the subwoofer is often below the noise. Scaling an unwrapped angle, anchored at the crossover, turns out to be equivalent once the bulk delay below has been removed, and worse when it has not.

The delay $T$ is what keeps the all-pass small. Advancing the subwoofer phasor by $T$ before its phase is taken leaves the all-pass carrying only the residual, while the bulk of the offset is applied as an ordinary delay on the main's output, where it costs nothing. $T$ is a relative quantity, not the delay applied to the main. If the outputs receive bulk delays $a_m$ and $a_s$, the phase difference left between the two acoustic paths through the crossover region is $2\pi f\,[\,T-(a_m-a_s)\,]$, which vanishes at every frequency only if
\begin{equation}
  T \;=\; a_m - a_s .
  \label{eq:aligncond}
\end{equation}
The group-alignment mode designs each speaker's correction with exactly its own $a_i - a_s$, so \eqref{eq:aligncond} holds for every speaker of the rig and for any trim applied to the subwoofer against the group.

$T$ is estimated from the measurements themselves, and the estimator has to be chosen with care, because the two available measures of lateness are not measuring the same quantity. An arrival time read from an impulse-response peak is accurate to about the inverse of the measured bandwidth \cite{Knapp1976}, some \SI{50}{\micro\second} for a full-range loudspeaker but \SI{10}{\milli\second} for a subwoofer that passes nothing above \SI{100}{\hertz}, so it is weakest exactly where it is wanted. The plugin instead fits the slope of the anchored subwoofer's unwrapped phase over $[\,f_x/2,\,2f_x\,]$ by least squares weighted by the measured power $|\bar S|^2$, which returns the effective group delay of the subwoofer in the crossover region, geometry and filtering together, and that is what decides whether the two sources add or cancel. Weighting each point of that fit by $|\bar S|^2$ lets the part of the band where the subwoofer actually has output decide the slope, which is what makes the estimate stable.

Because $A$ carries phase, the correction is a mixed-phase filter and is not causal on its own, which is why the exported impulse response is centred at $N/2$ and why it brings the bulk latency the compensation of Section~\ref{sec:latency} then absorbs. A minimum-phase export rebuilds the filter from $|C|$ alone and $A$ has unit magnitude, so the subwoofer alignment disappears from it entirely. The loudspeakers still arrive together in that mode, since the delays are written to the outputs regardless, but there is no phase steering through the crossover.

\section{Multi-speaker group alignment}\label{sec:group}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

For rigs of more than a couple of loudspeakers, a group-analysis mode holds one transfer-function estimate per speaker (up to 16, plus one shared subwoofer when the rig has one) side by side, sharing a single set of correction-design settings across all of them. A whole measurement folder is loaded in one step, the manifest supplying which channels were measured, which one is the subwoofer and which output each speaker sits on. Because every channel of a run was measured with the microphone in one place, each subwoofer capture is paired with the speaker capture of the same run, which keeps the pairing correct even when a speaker missed a run. Individual runs can be excluded from the analysis, and runs measured through an existing correction are excluded by default, since a correction has to be designed from the uncorrected loudspeaker.

Each speaker's bulk propagation delay, estimated as in Section~\ref{sec:correction}, is compared across the group and every driver is delayed to match the most distant one, so the whole rig arrives coherently at the reference position. The resulting delay is fed back into each speaker's own subwoofer phase-alignment term before its correction filter is finalised. A level-matching trim per speaker is also derived, as the mean power of its corrected response over the \SIrange{500}{2000}{\hertz} band that SMPTE ST 2095-1 specifies for channel level calibration \cite{SMPTE2095}, relative to the quietest speaker of the group. It is reported rather than applied, so that a deliberate level offset is never overwritten.

The subwoofer itself is never given a correction FIR filter. It contributes only its computed alignment delay and polarity (plus eventual gain lowpass order 2/4 crossover filter as given by the config tool or tuned manually in the matrix).

Applying the result exports every speaker's correction impulse response, writes the delays, the filters and the subwoofer's polarity onto the corresponding outputs, and saves a markdown report listing the settings, the files used, the measured and applied delays, the suggested trims and, optionally, the plotted responses. Loading, re-analysing and exporting a whole group run on a background thread pool with progress feedback, so the editor stays responsive with long, multi-position measurement sets across many speakers, and a batch already running survives the editor being closed.

\section{Validation}\label{sec:validation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

The alignment was evaluated on a two-way stereo rig with one subwoofer in a treated studio, crossed over at \SI{80}{\hertz}, the analysis running from \SI{40}{\hertz} and the correction rendered at 8192 taps. The loudspeakers and the reference position form a triangle with \SI{1.70}{\metre} sides and a \SI{2.55}{\metre} base, so the pair is wide and subtends nearly \SI{100}{\degree}, and the subwoofer stands on the floor between them, \SI{1.6}{\metre} from the reference position. Each main and the subwoofer were first measured on their own, with the correction bypassed, at ten microphone positions over the listening area: six spanning \SI{45}{\centi\metre} along the axis and \SI{34}{\centi\metre} across it, and four of them repeated \SI{8}{\centi\metre} higher, each with one \SI{15}{\second} synchronized sweep per channel. One group analysis was run on those captures and exported twice, linear phase and minimum phase. The system was then measured through the matrix at eight positions in three states, visited one after another at each position without moving the microphone: with no correction, with the linear-phase correction, and with the minimum-phase one.

Six of those eight positions have a counterpart in the first session and the other two sit \SI{8}{\centi\metre} above anything it visited. The subwoofer's routing level was set at \SI{-7.40}{\dB}, following what the analysis had suggested, and its polarity, normal in all three states.

Each alignment strategy is computed from a single set of recordings, the one in which each main and the subwoofer were measured on their own, and the summation it would produce is predicted from them. Every strategy is then scored on exactly the same data, and whatever separates two of them comes from the strategy rather than from the session. Because each subwoofer capture is delay-anchored on the main capture of the same position, the same propagation delay $d_p$ has been removed from both, and the acoustic sum at position $p$ is exactly
\begin{equation}
  Y_p(\omega) = e^{-j\omega T}\,H_{\mathrm{hp}}(\omega)\,C(\omega)\,H_p(\omega)
              \;\pm\; H_{\mathrm{lp}}(\omega)\,S_p(\omega)
  \label{eq:predsum}
\end{equation}
up to the common factor $e^{-j\omega d_p}$ that the anchoring took off both terms, which is a pure delay and changes no magnitude. Here $H_p$ and $S_p$ are the measured responses of the main and of the subwoofer at that position, and $H_{\mathrm{hp}}$, $H_{\mathrm{lp}}$ the bass-management filters. Nothing is simulated except the filters the plugin would itself apply. Every number reported below comes from a leave-one-position-out design, {\it i.e.} to score position $p$, the correction is derived from the nine other positions only and then evaluated at $p$, and this is repeated with each of the ten positions held out in turn, so no filter is ever scored on a measurement that helped shape it.

Figure~\ref{fig:validation}(a) compares the the corrected measurement divided by the uncorrected one taken at the same position with the applied filter magnitude. The minimum-phase export follows the filter it was designed from to $\sim$\SI{0.3}{\dB} RMS over \SIrange{200}{10000}{\hertz} on both channels. The linear-phase export follows it to $\sim$\SI{0.5}{\dB}, and the difference between the two is due to a defect of the delay line that reduces the treble about half a decibel. It has been since repaired by rounding the delay to whole samples and SuperMoTo now uses only integer delay samples. The peak samples of the designed IRs are $0.98$ and $1.72$ on the left and $1.42$ and $2.10$ on the right. If a correction boosts somewhere, every stage that stores it has to carry samples above full scale. That is why SuperMoTo stores the IRs internally as zipped 32bit float samples.

Below \SI{200}{\hertz}, the crossover region is checked against the prediction of~\eqref{eq:predsum} on Figure~\ref{fig:validation}(b) for the three measured states at once. Over \SIrange{35}{250}{\hertz} the model reproduces well the six measured cases.

The two exports are also the measured control on the alignment itself. They are rendered from one analysis, they are given the same output delay and the same subwoofer feed, and the minimum-phase rendering drops the designed phase and with it the all-pass (Section~\ref{sec:subalign}). At 8192 IR length they share the same magnitude, to \SI{0.06}{\dB} RMS over \SIrange{80}{200}{\hertz} and \SI{0.01}{\dB} above \SI{200}{\hertz}. The same pair rendered at 2048 taps differs from itself by \SI{1.63}{\dB} RMS across the crossover region, because neither rendering has room there for the structure the design asks for.

Four things follow. Correcting the main's magnitude on its own makes the summation worse than leaving the system alone, by \SI{0.3}{\dB}, because a minimum-phase magnitude correction moves the main's phase through the crossover region without regard for the subwoofer. Most of what is then recovered comes from the bulk delay, and the all-pass leaves \SI{-0.42}{\dB} of efficiency and a worst cancellation of \SI{1.1}{\dB} where the magnitude-corrected system loses \SI{4.9}{\dB} and the untouched one \SI{3.5}{\dB}. On the average it is not, on this rig, better than a magnitude correction given the arrival-time delay, which reaches \SI{-0.38}{\dB}. The two are within \SI{0.05}{\dB} of each other and each is the better of the pair at five of the ten positions on both mains, the aligned one running from \SI{-0.09}{\dB} at its best position to \SI{-1.06}{\dB} at its worst. What separates them is not the average but what it takes to get there, which is the subject of the next paragraph. And the leave-one-out scores match the in-sample ones to \SI{0.03}{\dB}, so the subwoofer's phase through the crossover behaves as a property of the room and the rig rather than of one seat, which is what justifies designing the alignment from a spatial average.

The all-pass is the strategy that does not need to be told anything. Inverting the subwoofer, with the correction redesigned against it as the plugin does when that switch is set, leaves the aligned result at \SI{-0.37}{\dB} against \SI{-0.42}{\dB} the right way up, where an alignment by delay alone falls to \SI{-6.1}{\dB}. Indeed, the all-pass can absorb the half turn, a delay cannot. Nor does it much care which estimator supplies the bulk delay, or whether one is supplied at all (dropping it entirely costs \SI{0.05}{\dB}) where the two estimators of Section~\ref{sec:subalign} disagree by \SI{0.7}{\milli\second} here and that choice is worth \SI{0.25}{\dB} to a delay-only alignment. This is also where the analysis band edge earns its setting. The correction fades to unity over a half-octave skirt below that edge and the alignment factor fades with it, so an edge at the crossover switches the steering off below $f_x/\sqrt2$, inside the region the subwoofer dominates. An earlier campaign run that way lost the polarity immunity entirely, the inverted case falling to \SI{-1.05}{\dB}. It is therefore best to define the analysis range so that it begins below the cutoff frequency rather than at that level; there is no downside to this, since the correction tapers off anyway.

Three limits should be stated. First, the comparison between strategies is predicted rather than measured; the measured pair above tests the one step the exports can isolate, and the rest of the table rests on the offline chain. Rendered at the same 8192 taps and compared like with like, that chain reproduces the plugin's own exported filters to between \SI{0.06}{\dB} and \SI{0.13}{\dB} RMS in every band from \SI{40}{\hertz} to \SI{8}{\kilo\hertz}, for both renderings and both mains, which is as close as two independent implementations of the same arithmetic are likely to come. Second, the geometry limits what the arrival-time estimator can mean: it places the subwoofer between \SI{1.45}{\metre} and \SI{1.75}{\metre} from the ten positions, a spread of \SI{29}{\centi\metre}, of which its impulse-response peak reports \SI{8}{\centi\metre}. The arrival time of a source band-limited to the crossover region is repeatable without being accurate, and on an earlier rig whose subwoofer arrival wandered by \SI{6.6}{\milli\second} across positions it was the crossover-band estimate that held; that it is the weaker of the two here is a property of this rig. Third, one rig with one subwoofer in one room is one case, and the numbers here are not a claim about rooms in general.

%One defect found along the way is worth recording, because it is the kind a validation of this sort is good at finding. A minimum-phase filter concentrates its energy in its first samples, so its peak sample is large, and the minimum-phase exports here peak \SI{4.7}{\dB} and \SI{6.4}{\dB} above digital full scale. \section{Implementation and availability}\label{sec:impl}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

%----------------------------------------------------------------------

SuperMoTo is written in C++ and built with CMake against the JUCE framework\footnote{\url{https://juce.com/}}. Its reusable parts (the correction and measurement DSP, the plotting components, the preset system) live in a separate library, FxmeTools\footnote{\url{https://github.com/odoare/FxmeTools}}, shared with the author's other plugins, and the parts that need no JUCE at all are compiled and unit-tested on their own. Per-output FIR convolution uses the WDL non-uniformly partitioned convolution engine\footnote{\url{https://www.cockos.com/wdl/}} \cite{Gardner1995}.

The engine holds no parameters of its own. It pulls them from the shared model whenever the model's version counter changes, under a try-lock that defers the pull to the next block if the editor happens to be writing, so a GUI edit or a state restore can never stall the audio callback, and every update path is written to allocate nothing on the audio thread. Impulse-response files are read, resampled and installed on the message thread under a lock that the audio thread only ever takes with a try-lock, so a load in flight costs a few unconvolved blocks rather than a blocked callback. Meters and the spectrum analyser are fed by lock-free taps placed on each crosspoint and each output.

Continuous-integration builds for Linux, macOS (Apple Silicon universal binary) and Windows are provided by a GitHub Actions workflow triggered by version tags. The plugin is available in VST3, AU, and Standalone formats with a fixed 32-in/32-out discrete channel bus, which sidesteps the inconsistent per-host discrete-channel-count negotiation of JUCE's AU and VST3 wrappers, the active matrix size being an independent, user-selectable setting up to that count. A user manual covering the workflow, the controls and the signal processing behind them is written in LaTeX alongside the source.

The source, the manual, the unit tests and the scripts that produce Table~\ref{tab:validation} and Figures~\ref{fig:validation} and~\ref{fig:firlength} are at \url{https://github.com/odoare/SuperMoTo}, under the AGPL~3.0 or commercial terms; the manual, the paper and the scripts themselves are under the LGPL~3.0.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

SuperMoTo puts the parts of a monitoring chain that are usually separate tools into one plugin instance, so that the routing, the per-loudspeaker correction, the time alignment and the Ambisonics decoding of a listening room are described in one place and calibrated without leaving it. The subwoofer alignment is the part that gains most from that arrangement. Because the measurement, the spatial average and the filter design live in the same instance, the alignment can be read straight off the measured phase in closed form and folded into the correction filter, where a tool working from outside the signal chain would have to fit a separate parametric all-pass to it.

What the approach cannot do is bounded by what any causal filter can do in a room. Cancellation notches and late reflections are not minimum-phase and are not inverted here, the correction is designed for the area the microphone positions span rather than for one specific posiiton, and the subwoofer alignment holds over that same area. Its effect on the summation through the crossover is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:validation}, where it leaves the two sources within \SI{0.4}{\dB} of coherent addition across a ten-position listening area.
